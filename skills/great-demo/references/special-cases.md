# Special-Case Demos

Non-standard demo formats that need their own playbook: trade show, large unqualified group, remote, scripted RFP, deployment, and canned demos.

## Trade Show Demos

Two frustrating scenarios you'll meet at booths.

1. Prospect walks up and says "show me your product"
2. Booth colleague brings an unqualified prospect over and says "show this person our product"

The rule in both cases: HOLD BACK. Don't move the mouse. Don't open anything.

Ask open-ended questions first. Open-ended means cannot be answered yes or no. The goal is uncovering CBI and Reasons before you touch the keyboard.

Best trade-show opener: "What are you most interested in accomplishing at this conference?" Open-ended, no easy way to brush it off.

If the prospect won't share enough to qualify, use the **Menu Approach**.

### The Menu Approach

You have minimal context: industry, company, possibly job title. You can't run a real Great Demo because you can't tie capabilities to a CBI you don't know.

Procedure:

1. Pick the Situation Slide and Illustration most likely to resonate based on what little you know.
2. Show it. Ask: "Is this what you want to see?"
3. Three outcomes:
   - "I have that exact problem" — go deeper into that scenario.
   - "Not exact, but similar" — go deeper.
   - "Don't have that problem" — move to the next item on the menu.
4. Present up to three to five Situation Slides and Illustrations before concluding there's no fit. Each takes about two minutes; three to five fits in ten minutes.

The Menu Approach is essentially a catalog. It saves time for both parties and increases the probability that something lands.

### The Handoff

Train booth colleagues to qualify briefly and then hand off to an SE professionally.

A good Handoff includes:

- Introduction of prospect to SE (and SE to prospect)
- Prospect's job function and main interests
- CBI, Reasons, and Specific Capabilities surfaced so far
- Time constraints ("only 15 minutes")

Professional Handoffs signal genuine interest in the prospect's situation. Prospects open up more as a result.

## Large Unqualified Groups

Scenario: you prepared for two or three qualified people. Many more showed up. Mixed roles, unknown needs.

Use the **To Do List** method.

### The To Do List

Procedure:

1. At the start, have each audience member introduce themselves: name, job function, and specifically what they want to see.
2. Capture every desired capability on a whiteboard as short phrase descriptions.
3. Tally duplicates as requests are repeated. Duplicates surface priority naturally.
4. Walk through the list verbally with the audience before demoing: mark which capabilities you have, which you don't, and which you'll demo today. Sets expectations clearly.
5. Two columns alongside the list: "product has this" and "will demo today."
6. Bias prioritization toward the needs of your initial contacts and high-ranking audience members.
7. Use generic demo material for segments where you have no customer-specific data; use prepared data where you do.
8. Treat it like a multi-solution demo: show ALL Illustrations first to hook every segment of the audience, then work through each capability segment.
9. Checkmark each To Do List item as completed. Same tracking mechanism as the Not Now List.
10. Final summary covers every item demonstrated.

The To Do List anecdote from the book: author arrived expecting two to three people, found 30+. While setting up, he casually polled the room. Then methodically asked each person what they wanted to see and built the whiteboard list. Walked the list with circle bullets for "will demo" and lines through "won't demo." Invited lined-out attendees to leave. Used a separate area of the whiteboard for the Not Now List. Checked off each item after demonstrating. Offered an optional Q&A stay. Some audience members asked to "run some searches" with their real data. Thirty minutes of ad-hoc problem-solving with their actual data. Result: got the order, substantially larger than originally expected.

The To Do List is also useful for large product roll-outs, internal training sessions, and any meeting where the audience composition wasn't fully predictable.

## Remote Demos

Cohan, writing in the mid-2000s, called Remote Demos a last-resort format. That framing has flipped. By 2026 remote is the default for a large share of B2B software demos, especially in early-stage discovery and follow-up rounds. Face-to-face is now reserved for high-stakes moments: critical evaluations, executive readouts, on-site implementation kickoffs.

What hasn't changed: facial expressions, puzzled looks, and unspoken questions all get filtered through the camera. Customers are less likely to speak up. Engagement drops unless you actively counter it.

The principle still applies: prefer in-person for the highest-stakes moments. For everything else, lean into remote-native technique rather than treating it as a degraded fallback.

When the demo really matters and you can't get on-site, two patterns work. First, send one person (account rep or salesperson) to the customer's site to manage the room while the SE demonstrates remotely; the customer stays engaged because someone is physically present. Second, leverage an established relationship and prior credibility so the audience already trusts the screen.

### Generating Interactivity

The name of the game in Remote Demos is INTERACTIVITY. The better you engage, the higher your probability of success.

**Outbound interactivity tools:**

- Toggle between the Demonstration Roadmap (a slide showing the agenda) and the live demo. Keeps the audience oriented. Breaks the demo into consumable segments.
- Use pen, pointer, or highlight tools in the collaboration software. New graphics appearing dynamically wake people up.

**Bidirectional interactivity tools:**

- **Polling.** Audience votes; everyone sees aggregate results. Inherently curious about what others think.
- **Give control of your desktop to a volunteer.** They drive for a while. Proves the software is easy to use. Best with your Champion; brief them beforehand to reduce risk.
- **Ask verbal questions and require responses.** For large groups, use the chat function.
- **Encourage questions throughout.** Use a shared document visible to all participants (Google Doc, shared Word file, or built-in collaboration whiteboard) to capture Not Now List questions. Strikethrough the entry once you've answered it. Visible progress reinforces the discipline.

### Preparing for a Remote Demo

- Verify customer firewalls and network connection at least one day before.
- Drill the collaboration tool's specific features (pointer, highlighter, chat, whiteboard, screen sharing, breakout rooms) in a session with a colleague. Know them cold; don't fumble live.
- Set screen resolution to a level that scales well across participant displays (many tools scale down, but excessive native resolution forces participants to squint).
- Monitor the demo on a separate device to see exactly what the audience sees. Often surprising.
- Use a wired headset with microphone, or a quality USB mic. Laptop speakers and built-in mics are poor; voice drops when you turn your head.
- Camera on by default. A visible presenter holds attention better than a faceless voice. Frame the shot so you're well-lit and centered.
- Clean your desktop, browser tabs, notification settings, and File Open dialogs before the demo. Don't expose which other companies you've been pitching. Disable Slack, Teams, Outlook, and personal-message notifications.
- Consider dedicating a separate environment to Remote Demos: a clean user profile, a separate browser, a dedicated VM, or a fresh demo tenant. Always ready, always clean.

### During the Remote Demo

- Have your best, most compelling screen up and ready the moment you start sharing. Never start on a login screen.
- Move the mouse very slowly. There is lag. Fast movements appear ragged on the customer's screen.
- Ask questions as you go to test whether the audience is still with you.
- Do not point at your own screen with your finger. The customer cannot see your hand.
- Summarize early and often. Use the Demonstration Roadmap as a structured opportunity for summary.

## Scripted RFP Demos

Common in government and enterprise RFP scenarios. The customer demands strict adherence to a predefined script that often doesn't reflect natural product flow.

The tension: you want to demonstrate business value and natural workflow. The customer wants to "level the playing field" with apples-to-apples vendor comparisons.

Rule one: respect the customer's process. Subverting it gets you disqualified immediately.

First move: try to renegotiate the script to better match product flow. Rarely succeeds. If it does, bias the renegotiated version toward Great Demo! format.

If the customer insists on the script as written:

1. Use the Introduction time to present Situation Slides and Illustrations addressing the real business objectives. Cut the corporate overview short to free up this time.
2. Create the vision first; then follow the script religiously in the body.
3. The Situation Slide and Illustration plant the why before the what. The script then plays out as proof points against an established frame.

The book's noted outcome: vendors who used intro time for a complete Situation Slide, Illustration, and Do It sequence, then followed the script religiously, won in several cases against vendors who only followed the script.

## Deployment Demos

Post-sale risk: "shelf-ware." Company buys 100 seats, rolls out 25, 75 sit unused. Future expansion sale: near zero.

Three root causes:

1. Vendor sold a vision the software can't fulfill. No fix available; this is a sales-integrity problem.
2. Sales team moved on after close, never handed CBI/Reason/Specific Capability context to implementation and training teams. **Fix:** ensure this context is explicitly transferred at handoff.
3. Training delivers generic "how-to" with no regard for specific user needs. **Fix:** apply the Great Demo! method to training. Start with "what" the software does for users before "how-to."

Deployment demos must show clear value up-front. Users need motivation before they'll invest energy in learning mechanics. Open with the Illustration. Connect every "how-to" segment back to the user's CBI.

Post-sale deployment success drives future expansion sales and reference stories. As important as the initial demo.

## Canned Demos

Pre-prepared demos using a Generic Demo format, run without live software. Inherently risky because you can't adjust to audience needs.

Major problem: often run without you present, so you can't diagnose CBI/Reasons or address gaps as they appear.

When canned demos make sense:

- Web-based or in-product interactive walkthroughs
- Screen recordings (MP4, embedded video)
- Click-through demos built in interactive demo platforms (Navattic, Storylane, Reprise, Demostack and similar)
- Demos for non-technical staff
- Hardware or network-constrained situations

### Building a Canned Demo

Web-based: create a range of demos organized by CBI/Reason/Specific Capability. Use lead-in pages that describe the business need before diving into the demo. Format options run from static screen captures with annotation (fastest to build) to recorded video walkthroughs (more immersive) to interactive click-through demos that let the viewer drive (highest engagement, more build effort).

Canned demo structure:

- At minimum: steps 1 and 2 (Illustration with summary, Do It with summary).
- Add steps 3 and 4 (Do It Again and Q&A) only if someone from your company will be present to control playback.
- Summarize, summarize, summarize. Since you aren't there, every summary is critical for keeping the unsupervised audience on track.
- Include a call to action in the summary: contact form link, phone number, URL for next step.

### Warnings

- Stale canned demos are worse than no demo. Keep web demos updated as the product and audience evolve.
- Anything distributed publicly is also visible to competitors. Decide deliberately what to reveal.
- Always map the capabilities shown to the specific needs of your target audience. A generic capability tour, distributed widely, is the worst outcome: it teaches nothing and signals you don't understand who your customers are.

## Generic Demos

A demo to an unqualified audience. Always carries more risk than a qualified demo because you don't know which capabilities will resonate.

When you must give one (trade shows, product launches, broad webinars):

1. Lead with multiple Situation Slides covering the most common CBIs for your typical buyer. Treat it as a Menu Approach.
2. Build Illustrations matched to each Situation Slide.
3. Run a multi-solution structure: present all Illustrations upfront to hook every audience segment, then walk each capability segment in turn.
4. Use show-of-hands or chat polls to surface which segments matter most to the audience in front of you.
5. Always close with a call to action and contact information.

A Generic Demo that converts is one that gets unqualified audience members to self-identify which Situation Slide describes them. Use that signal to triage the most engaged participants for follow-up.
