# Live-Demo Delivery

How to handle questions, language, mouse movement, props, and recovery during a live demo.

## Three Types of Questions

Every question that comes up during a live demo falls into one of three categories. The category determines how you handle it.

**Great Questions**: directly impact the Specific Capabilities being demonstrated and are relevant to what's on screen right now. Answering them supports and underscores the flow.

- Action: answer right away. Briefly. A simple "Yes" or "No" is often what the customer wants. Clamp your mouth shut if a one-word answer satisfies them.
- Tell: if a question needs more than two sentences to answer, it's not a Great Question. It's a Good Question. You can feel the difference.
- Reward: praise good questions and crack a joke if the moment fits. ("I'm really glad you asked that. That's the next capability I'd planned to share. And that's another twenty bucks I owe you!")

**Good Questions**: meaningful but not relevant to what's on screen now. The risk isn't difficulty; the risk is the chain reaction. Answering pulls you off pathway. Side discussions break out. Other audience members re-ignite internal debates ("Does it run on Oracle?" can trigger an Oracle-vs-Postgres argument that derails the entire room).

- Action: defer to the Not Now List.

**Stupid Questions**: questions that should never have been asked. Two sources: hostile audience members trying to show off or trip you up, and audience members who genuinely don't understand.

- Action: handle exactly like a Good Question. Place it on the Not Now List with equal respect.

There are only Great Questions and Good Questions, as far as you're concerned. The Stupid Question category exists to remind you that people will ask them, and you need a method that works regardless. Treating a Stupid Question with respect satisfies the hostile questioner and silences them without confrontation.

The more technical the audience, the more Good and Stupid Questions you'll get.

## The Not Now List

The Not Now List is the question-management tool. Use it for every Good and Stupid Question.

Script:

> "That's an excellent question. I promise to address it, but for right now I'd like to continue with our meeting plan. Let me write it down here, on the whiteboard. We'll make sure to address it later. Is that OK with you?"

Four keys to making it work:

1. **Honestly acknowledge.** It must be sincere. If the questioner senses you're blowing them off, you must answer immediately. Insincerity is worse than a derailed demo.
2. **Write it down publicly.** Whiteboard, flipchart, or a shared on-screen document. The questioner must see you've captured it clearly.
3. **Ask "Is that OK with you?"** Lets the questioner participate in the decision to postpone. This is crucial. Without it, you're imposing.
4. **You must answer it later.** Failing to answer leaves an open wound that can cost the sale. Always close the loop in Q&A.

By the end of the demo you may have six or more questions queued. That's good. They become the Q&A agenda.

## The Q&A Section

Address Not Now List questions in the formal Q&A, after the final summary of the last Do It Again.

If the demo is the last item on the meeting agenda: announce after the final summary that you'll now address the Not Now List. Invite interested parties to stay; let others leave. This triages the audience naturally. The most engaged stay. Hostile questioners often leave at this point. Plan this option in advance with the Selling Team.

Process for answering each question:

1. Restate the question and confirm understanding ("If I understand correctly, you're asking... Is that right?")
2. Provide the answer crisply but sufficiently
3. Summarize both the question and the answer

Restating identifies the questioner and turns the answer into a personalized 1:1 dialog. Follow-up questions become natural extensions of that dialog rather than free-for-all interruptions.

After all Not Now List items are addressed, ask if there are further questions. Then close the Q&A by walking through the list one more time, putting a checkmark next to each as you go. This is visual closure.

## "Can You Show Me?"

A Not Now List item that asks for a live demonstration. Two paths.

**You can.** Run a mini-Great Demo: Illustration, brief summary, Do It, brief summary, Do It Again if needed, summary, Q&A. May only be one to two minutes total. Test at each step whether the Illustration alone closes the question. If yes, summarize and return to the Not Now List.

**You can't.** Two sub-cases.

- The software has the capability but you can't demo it now. Say so. Verbal confirmation may suffice. If not, use static materials (printed Illustrations, web pages, whiteboard sketch). If a live demo is truly required to close the issue, schedule a follow-up.
- The software doesn't have the capability. Say so honestly. Liars are always caught out. Offer an alternative if available. If it's a "nice to have," offer to sponsor the request with Development. If it's a Specific Capability genuinely needed to close the sale: tell the Salesperson immediately, document the requirement carefully, and report it to Development.

A missing capability isn't necessarily a loss. If the customer trusts you to deliver it, they may still buy. Investing time to define the requirement with them is competitive differentiation.

## "Can You Run My Example?"

A genuine opportunity. Run it during Q&A if you're confident the result will be useful.

Set expectations carefully first. Start at zero, build upward. State possible outcomes including the bad ones. Nothing should be a surprise.

If it works: exceptional impact. Audience members leave with real results from their own data. They become supporters because they got value before buying.

Counterintuitive note: a search returning no results can be a positive. Three reasons: (1) the information is genuinely new (patent or publishing opportunity), (2) they no longer have to keep searching with old tools for the same negative result, (3) finding the negative was much faster with your product. Reframe the negative.

## Language

Match technical level to the audience. Too high alienates. Too low undermines perceived competence.

Acronyms: state the full term first three times, then use the acronym. If the audience clearly doesn't know the terms, avoid them entirely.

Subtle word choices that matter:

| Avoid | Prefer |
|---|---|
| "I'll show you..." | "I'd like to share with you..." (collaborative) |
| "This will allow you to..." | "This will enable you to..." (stronger) |
| "I showed..." (in summary) | "We explored..." (received more favorably) |
| "but" | "and" ("but" is heard as "no, I disagree") |

## The Buzzword List

Words that erode credibility:

robust, powerful, flexible, integrated, seamless, extensible, scalable, interoperable, easy-to-use, intuitive, user-friendly, comprehensive, best-of-breed, world-class, cutting-edge

Replace with concrete facts:

- "10,000 users in day-to-day production worldwide" beats "robust"
- "99.98% uptime on a 24/7/365 basis" beats "reliable"
- "3 clicks to complete the task" beats "easy-to-use"

Test for borderline phrases: ask "in whose opinion?" If the answer is Marketing or "us," rephrase with concrete facts or attributed customer quotes.

"Seamless" and "integrated" are the worst offenders. If integration were truly seamless, there would be no integration industry.

## Pace

Slow down. You know the software far better than the audience. They need time to follow.

The mouse is your voice on screen. Make every motion deliberate. Use it to point, underscore, highlight, circle, link items. No random motion; it's distracting.

When stepping away from the keyboard, leave the mouse pointing at the most important item on screen.

Fast, erratic movement makes the software look complex and hostile. Slow, deliberate movement makes it look logical and easy.

In remote demos, move the mouse very slowly. There is lag. Fast movements appear ragged on the customer's screen. Complex graphics make it worse.

## Screen Pointing

Options ranked by usefulness:

- Mouse cursor: best when you're at the keyboard
- Hands: excellent natural pointers; can point, surround, underline, trace
- Annotation tools built into presentation or screen-share software: pen, highlighter, spotlight, on-screen pointer; use them deliberately, not constantly
- Laser pointer: only for large rooms or non-computer media; amplifies any nervous tremor (one millimeter of hand shake equals half a meter on the conference screen)

If using a laser pointer and you're nervous: use two hands, move deliberately, leave it on long enough to make the point, never flash on/off rapidly.

## Props

Props illustrate, ground the abstract, keep audiences awake, and differentiate you from competitors. Software companies rarely think about them.

The book's best example: an SE walked into a meeting carrying a large pile of books, sat down, sighed, said "I really hate having to search through all these books to find the information I need. I wish there was a better way..." Then looked up and said "Hey, I wonder if this new software can do what they claim?" Then did exactly that.

Exercise: for each product, develop at least one prop for each key application.

## Recovering from Bugs and Crashes

Bugs happen. Two axioms:

- "All useful software has bugs."
- "The pain and embarrassment potential for any bug appearing is directly proportional to the importance of the demo. Corollary: the severity of the bug that appears is also directly proportional to the importance of the demo."

Three bug categories.

**Cosmetic bugs**: screen repainting, cursor problems, wrong colors. Don't affect functionality.

- Action: ignore and continue smoothly. Change screens momentarily to clear visual debris. Audience may never notice.

**Serious bugs**: functionality is unusable. Broken searches, error messages, unimplemented features.

- A serious bug WILL repeat if you retry the same path. Don't retry. Retrying proves you don't know the software AND it doesn't work.
- Action: (1) acknowledge the bug matter-of-factly, short and concise; (2) show what should have happened using your printed Illustration or other proof; (3) pick up the demo at a point immediately after the bug and continue.
- "Insanity is doing the same thing over and over again and expecting a different result." Don't be insane.
- After the demo: privately test the offending capability during Q&A. If it now works, decide whether to revisit. If not, schedule a brief Remote Demo follow-up.

**Crashes**: handle like a serious bug, but also fill the reboot time productively.

- In a Selling Team: the Salesperson takes the lead. Returns to the Roadmap, summarizes, addresses Not Now List questions. The SE quietly restores the system. Blank the display while rebooting (don't lock the cable down — being able to pull it or kill the wireless cast quickly prevents the audience from watching the boot sequence). SE signals readiness. Clean handoff back.
- Solo: shift to printed materials. Talk through the Illustration and the Delta. Do not narrate the reboot.

Stay calm in all bug and crash situations. Even if you're sweating, don't let them see you sweat. Confidence under pressure neutralizes hostile audience members; visible nerves attract them.

For Alpha or Beta product demos: tell the audience upfront to expect bugs. That's part of what these releases are for. Setting the expectation reduces the impact when one appears.

## Humor

Be human. Interject humor during the demo, not necessarily at the opening.

Safe targets, in order:

1. Yourself
2. Your Selling Team colleagues
3. Generally, never the audience (exception: long, trusted relationship with established give-and-take)

Best moment for humor: a crash, bug, or hardware problem. It fills dead air. The book's anecdote: a presenter's PC froze, he calmly started the reboot, told the "three engineers in a car" software joke, and the computer was ready by the punch line.

## Confidence and Poise

Confidence comes from natural talent or from practice. Knowing exactly what and how to demo (the structural foundation this skill provides) gives you the knowledge base. Practice converts knowledge into poise.

Effective question management is the primary tool for demonstrating poise under pressure. The Not Now List protects you from being knocked off-balance.
