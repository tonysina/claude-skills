## Why it still reads as AI-written

The essay is built like a five-act lesson plan: incident, escalation, turning point, payoff, moral. Every paragraph does exactly one job, in order, and nothing is left over at the end. Humans writing about their own lives almost never manage that, and readers can feel it even when they can't name it.

The specific structural tells:

- **Perfect chronology.** Monday, two weeks, a Thursday in the third week, the following week, the next review, then reflection. No jump-cuts, no memory intruding out of order, no backfill. Real recollection doesn't arrive in sequence.
- **The setup echoes in the conclusion.** "Eleven months" in sentence one comes back as "the fear I carried for eleven months" in the last paragraph. That bookend is a structural signature, not a style one.
- **Emotional beats on a schedule.** Stomach drops, chest tightens, heat rises up the neck. Three body signals, one per act, escalating in order. It reads as instrumentation rather than memory.
- **Everything that could complicate the lesson is pre-emptively closed.** The pipeline slip has no consequence. The contractor "did it better" so no loose end there. Dana's motive is fully explained in one sentence. Nothing costs anything.
- **The narrator explains the joke.** The performance review line is a good ending beat, but "which made me laugh, because all I had done was ask a question" tells the reader what to feel about it.
- **The moral is stated as a thesis.** "What I learned is that..." followed by "everything at work got easier, and I have never gone back." Total resolution, zero residue. This is the single biggest tell.
- **Dana is a function, not a person.** Her only detail is "the particular brightness she reserved for asking favours," which exists to serve the theme. No detail in the essay is there just because it was true.
- **Uniform paragraph rhythm.** Six paragraphs of roughly equal weight. No long messy one, no one-liner.

## Structural rewrite

Same facts, same voice, same length. What changed is the order, what gets explained, and what gets left open.

> Dana's review that year said I had "developed strong prioritisation judgment." I still have the PDF. I've read the line a few times since and it still seems to be describing someone else, because what actually happened was that I asked her one question, once, and she answered it in about two seconds.
>
> I should say what I was like before that. Eleven months in, twenty-six, and I had never once told anyone at that company what I couldn't do. When Dana came to my desk on a Monday and said the client wanted the reporting dashboard three weeks early, I said yes before she'd finished the sentence. I was already late on the data pipeline rebuild and I was on call that week. None of that came out of my mouth. I remember looking at the calendar after she left and not being able to make the squares mean anything.
>
> The dashboard shipped. I worked until nine most nights to do it, which sounds worse than it was; the office was quiet after seven and I liked it. The pipeline slipped a month. Nobody mentioned the pipeline, and I still don't know whether that was because nobody noticed or because someone had decided it wasn't worth mentioning to me. I'd like to know. I've never asked.
>
> When she came back with the second dashboard, the executive one, the answer that came out was not a no. It was: "I can do that, or I can finish the pipeline. Which one do you want?" I don't remember deciding to say it. I hadn't rehearsed anything. Dana blinked, said "the pipeline, obviously," and went off to find someone else. The someone else was a contractor who built the thing in less time than I would have taken and, I think, built it better, which I did not enjoy finding out.
>
> So she'd never wanted both. Nobody had told her she couldn't have both, so she'd assumed. That's the version I tell people, anyway, when the subject comes up. It's true as far as it goes.
>
> What it leaves out is that I said yes again about four months later, to a different manager, for the same reason, and worked the same nine o'clocks. The question is easy to ask once you know it exists. It just turns out you have to keep asking it, and some weeks I still don't.

## What each change is doing

- **Opens at the review, not the Monday.** Starting from the end and backfilling breaks the lesson-plan chronology. The reader now has to assemble the sequence, which is what memory feels like.
- **The "eleven months" echo is gone.** It appears once, as a fact, and never returns as a callback.
- **Body signals cut from three to zero.** The one physical detail kept is the calendar, and it's about perception failing rather than an organ reacting.
- **One thing is left genuinely unresolved.** Whether anyone noticed the pipeline slip. The narrator admits not knowing and not asking. That's residue, and residue is human.
- **The contractor detail now costs something.** "Which I did not enjoy finding out" replaces the magnanimous version.
- **The narrator distrusts their own tidy explanation.** "That's the version I tell people" flags the Dana-never-wanted-both line as a story the narrator tells, not the truth of the matter.
- **A non-thematic detail is added.** Liking the quiet office after seven doesn't serve the lesson. It slightly undercuts it. That's the point.
- **The ending refuses the moral.** No "what I learned." Instead an admission that the lesson didn't stick cleanly, and still doesn't. The last sentence is unfinished on purpose.
- **Paragraph weights vary.** One long paragraph, one three-sentence one, no symmetry.

If you want to keep the original's more triumphant ending, the minimum structural fix is still: break the chronology, cut the "what I learned" paragraph, and leave one thing open. Those three alone will move it most of the way.
