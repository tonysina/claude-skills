You're right that it's too neat, and the neatness is mostly structural, not word-level. A few things are doing it:

- **The four-label template.** Challenge / Solution / Results / Looking ahead is the shape every vendor case study has, so the reader's brain files it as marketing before reading a sentence. Nothing in the piece is allowed to happen outside its assigned box.
- **Every problem introduced gets resolved.** Forty minutes becomes six, overtime becomes under two hours, fuel drops 11 percent. Three problems, three solutions, nothing left hanging. Real rollouts leave a loose end.
- **The quote is placed where a quote goes.** It arrives after the numbers, as a warm human garnish, then the piece moves on. It's actually the most interesting line in the doc and it's being used as decoration.
- **The ending states the moral.** "The lesson from the rollout is clear" followed by an aphorism followed by "transformed its entire operating rhythm." That's the tidy bow. A person who actually lived through this would not summarize it that way.
- **Maria Chen's feelings are asserted, not shown.** "Felt the frustration in her chest" is a novelistic tell the writer added; it's the kind of thing a copywriter puts in because the brief said "make it human."

Here's a restructured version. I've kept every fact and number from the original and haven't invented any new ones. Where the piece needs real texture that only Ridgeline can supply, I've marked it in brackets rather than making something up.

**Ridgeline Logistics stopped starting its mornings on the phone**

For years the first forty minutes of every workday at Ridgeline Logistics were spent waiting. Three regional coordinators, each with their own spreadsheet, would phone each other to sort out which of the company's 140 vans had been booked onto overlapping routes. Drivers sat in the yard until the calls ended. Everyone knew the routine. Nobody liked it, and nobody had a better idea that didn't involve one of the three coordinators giving up their spreadsheet.

Operations director Maria Chen had walked past those idle vans enough times that she'd stopped noticing them, which is its own kind of problem.

The fix, when it came in March 2025, was not dramatic: a shared scheduling board that all three regions could see at once. Route conflicts show up flagged on the board instead of surfacing in a phone call. Drivers get their assignments on their phones the night before, so the yard is moving by the time the coordinators sit down.

What Chen didn't expect was how fast the drivers went for it. "Honestly, I didn't expect the drivers to take to it so quickly," she said. "By the second week they were asking why we hadn't done this years ago." That question doesn't have a comfortable answer, and she doesn't offer one.

The rollout itself took six weeks. Two of those were spent running the new board alongside the old spreadsheets, which meant coordinators were maintaining both for a fortnight. [If there was a moment during parallel running where the two systems disagreed and someone had to decide which one to trust, that belongs here. It's the most believable part of any rollout and it's currently missing.]

The numbers from the first quarter:

- Average morning departure delay went from forty minutes to six.
- Fuel spend fell 11 percent, mostly because routes stopped overlapping.
- Coordinator overtime, which had run around twelve hours a week per person, dropped to under two.

The overtime figure is the one Chen brings up first. The other two are the ones that end up in the deck.

Next year Ridgeline wants to put maintenance scheduling on the same board. Whether that goes as smoothly is an open question; maintenance has its own coordinators and its own spreadsheets, and they weren't the ones asking why this hadn't been done years ago.

**What changed structurally**

1. **Dropped the section labels.** The piece now moves as a single account, so the reader isn't told in advance where the problem ends and the solution starts.
2. **Opened in the middle of the routine** rather than with a company descriptor. The "140 vans across the Pacific Northwest" fact is still there, it's just inside the scene instead of a preamble.
3. **Moved the quote earlier and let it carry weight.** It now sits before the metrics, and the follow-on line ("that question doesn't have a comfortable answer") lets a small uncomfortable note stay in the piece.
4. **Put the metrics in a list and then undercut them lightly.** "The other two are the ones that end up in the deck" acknowledges the genre it's in, which reads as a person talking rather than a template filling itself.
5. **Replaced the moral with an open question.** The original closed with "the lesson is clear" and a line about the whole operating rhythm being transformed. The new ending points at the next rollout and admits it might not go the same way. Nothing is claimed that the facts don't support.
6. **Removed the "frustration in her chest" line** and replaced it with a quieter, more plausible observation (she'd stopped noticing the vans). That's an editorial choice you may want to check with Chen.

**Two things I want to flag**

- The bracketed note about parallel running is a real gap. Every restructured version of this piece will still feel slightly smooth until there's one honest detail about something that didn't work the first time. Ask Ridgeline for it.
- I added two small inferences that aren't in the source: that Chen brings up the overtime figure first, and that maintenance has its own coordinators and spreadsheets. Both are plausible and both make the piece more human, but they're mine, not Ridgeline's. Confirm or cut them before this goes out.
