The neatness is structural, and it comes from three places: the piece explains its own lesson at the end, nothing in it costs anything or stays open, and its one emotional beat is routed through the body. I rewrote at the section level to fix those three and left the sentences alone.

## Mode

At 267 words this is short-form work collateral, so I applied the lighter-touch rules: thematic restraint, resolution mode, and emotional expression. I skipped temporal reordering and subplots, which the skill reserves for longer pieces. The case-study section skeleton (challenge, solution, results, looking ahead) is a genre convention, so I kept it.

## Findings

Ordered by how strongly each pattern separates AI from human writing.

1. **Explicit lesson at the end.** "The lesson from the rollout is clear: when the people who do the work can see the same picture, coordination stops being a daily negotiation and becomes a habit. For Ridgeline, that shift has transformed not just its mornings but its entire operating rhythm." The narrator states the theme and then restates it as a transformation claim. Narratorial thematic commentary runs 77% in AI writing versus 52% in human writing, and an explicit moral at the close scores 3.94 versus 3.28. This is the single biggest source of the too-neat feel.

2. **Nothing unresolved, nothing costly.** Every metric improves. The six-week rollout is described only by its length. "Looking ahead" is a second victory rather than an open question. Human writing tolerates mixed or ambivalent outcomes far more often (59% versus 38%), and AI narratives resolve through a clean causal chain where each step tidily produces the next.

3. **Embodied emotion.** "Operations director Maria Chen felt the frustration in her chest every time she walked past the idle vans." It is the only emotional moment in the piece, and it goes through the body. Physical-sensation emotion appears in 81% of AI writing versus 38% of human writing. One such moment is normally fine, but here it is 100% of the emotional register.

4. **Total thematic unity.** Every section, and the title ("From Spreadsheet Chaos to Coordinated Scheduling"), serves the same message. High thematic unity scores 4.74 in AI writing versus 4.41 in human writing.

Human-elevated signals that are absent: no reader address, no moral ambiguity. Both are listed below as optional moves I did not apply.

## Rewrite

Ridgeline Logistics: From Spreadsheet Chaos to Coordinated Scheduling

The challenge

Ridgeline Logistics runs 140 delivery vans across the Pacific Northwest. Until 2025, dispatch scheduling lived in a set of spreadsheets maintained by three regional coordinators. Every morning began the same way: coordinators phoned each other to reconcile overlapping routes, and drivers waited in the yard until the calls ended. On a typical day, vans left forty minutes late. Operations director Maria Chen was frustrated every time she walked past the idle vans.

The solution

In March 2025, Ridgeline rolled out a shared scheduling platform across all three regions. Coordinators now see one live board. Route conflicts are flagged automatically, and drivers get their assignments on their phones the night before. The rollout took six weeks. For two of those weeks the coordinators kept the old spreadsheets running alongside the new board, which meant maintaining both.

The results

Within the first quarter, average morning departure delay fell from forty minutes to six. Fuel spend dropped 11 percent because routes stopped overlapping. Coordinator overtime, which had run at roughly twelve hours a week per person, fell to under two.

"Honestly, I didn't expect the drivers to take to it so quickly," Chen said. "By the second week they were asking why we hadn't done this years ago."

Looking ahead

Maintenance scheduling still runs on spreadsheets. Ridgeline plans to move it onto the platform in 2026.

## What changed and why

**Intervention 1: removed the stated lesson.** The final paragraph no longer tells the reader what the story means. The ending is now a fact about what is still unfinished, and Chen's quote is left to carry the thematic weight on its own. Her line already says "why we hadn't done this years ago," which is the lesson in a character's voice rather than the narrator's.

**Intervention 2: opened one thread and surfaced one cost.** "Looking ahead" now begins with what has not been done (maintenance is still on spreadsheets) rather than with a plan. In the solution section, "two weeks of parallel running" became "kept the old spreadsheets running alongside the new board, which meant maintaining both," so the rollout has a visible burden instead of just a duration. Both are derived from facts already in the source. Neither is editorialized.

**Intervention 3: emotion as a direct label.** "Felt the frustration in her chest" became "was frustrated." Same fact, same person, no body. The piece now has one direct label (Chen's frustration) and one behavioral cue (drivers asking why this took so long), which is the mix human writers tend to produce.

## Downstream effects to watch

- **The ending works harder now.** With the moral gone, the last two sentences are the whole close. If marketing needs a takeaway line, put it outside the narrative as a pull-quote or sidebar rather than back in the narrator's voice.
- **Two derived facts need a check.** "Which meant maintaining both" and "still runs on spreadsheets" are inferred from the source, not stated in it. Confirm both with Ridgeline before publishing.
- **The title still announces the arc.** "From Chaos to Coordinated" states the resolution before the piece begins. I left it because titles are usually template-mandated, but it is the one remaining place the narrator explains the story.

## Optional moves I did not apply

- **A result that did not move.** The strongest anti-neat move is one metric that stayed flat or got worse, or one thing the platform made harder. I could not add this without inventing a fact about a real customer. If one exists, a single unexplained sentence in the results section would do more than everything above.
- **One line of reader address.** For example, opening the challenge with a "you" aimed at anyone who has run a yard. Direct address is a strong humanizing signal in professional writing (human 0.28 versus AI 0.07), but it changes the register of a case study, so it is your call.
- **A light temporal reorder.** Opening on Chen's "why we hadn't done this years ago" and then going back to the spreadsheets would introduce delayed disclosure. The skill skips this at short-form length, so I did not apply it, but it is the next move if you want to go further.
