That's not a dodge, so here's the reasoning.

At 91 words the budget is one figure. You already spent it — the last sentence runs one verb across two unlike objects: "It cost us about four hundred signups and a lesson I keep having to relearn." A concrete noun and an abstract one hanging off the same "cost." That's zeugma, and it's the best line in the paragraph. Anything I added would have to compete with it.

The rest is already doing the work a figure would be brought in to do:

**The contrast engine is running.** "Good thing." is two words between a 24-word sentence and a 38-word one. The ear catches differences, not qualities, and that's the difference. A figure dropped in near it would blunt the effect, not add to it.

**The diction is Saxon throughout.** shipped, watch, doubled, caught, fixed, cost. No nominalization chains, nothing to convert back into verbs. There's no free diction work to do here.

**The ending lands on a stress.** "relearn" takes the stress on the second syllable and it's a Saxon root. The closing clause fits in one breath. Both ear checks pass.

**The claims are correctly hedged.** "about four hundred signups," "half of Europe." Those are exactly the qualifiers a figure would eat. The most likely outcome of applying rhetoric here is that "about" disappears for rhythm and you've overstated your own post-mortem.

One thing I considered and rejected: there's a real antithesis latent in "it took us until Thursday to find out why" against "We fixed it in an hour" — two days to diagnose, sixty minutes to repair. Both poles are real and it survives the swap test. But putting them in matched frames would mean moving the fix next to the diagnosis, which collapses your chronology. The paragraph is built on the calendar — Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday — and the figure would cost you the spine to gain a sentence. Not worth it.
