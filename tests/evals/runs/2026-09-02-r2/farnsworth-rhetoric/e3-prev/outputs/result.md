**Original**

> Contoso: A comprehensive enterprise architecture platform enabling organizations to achieve strategic alignment through integrated visualization and collaborative governance capabilities.

Twenty-two words, eleven of them Latinate, and it ends on "capabilities" — the softest possible landing. The real content is two mechanisms (people can see the architecture; people govern it together) pointed at one outcome (the business moves in one direction). Everything else is padding.

Budget for a tagline is **one figure, or none**. Here are four treatments.

---

**1. Compression only — no figure**

> Contoso: enterprise architecture the whole business can see and steer.

Nothing rhetorical. Nominalizations back to verbs (*visualization* → see, *governance* → steer), the filler adjectives cut, and the line ends on a stressed Saxon monosyllable. Eleven words carrying every claim the original made.

**2. Isocolon — two members**

> Contoso: architecture you can see, decisions you can share.

Trigger: two items of equal weight that take equal grammatical shape. Load-bearing test passes — "see" is the visualization claim, "share" is the collaborative-governance claim, and cutting either loses information. Ends on "share."

**3. Isocolon — three members, asyndeton**

> Contoso: see the architecture, share the decisions, move as one.

Three is the number that resolves. Each member maps to a distinct claim in the source — visualization, governance, alignment — so it clears the load-bearing test rather than reading as a decorative triad. Conjunctions dropped for speed. Ends on "one."

**4. Antithesis**

> Contoso: a diagram doesn't align a business. A shared decision does.

Trigger: both poles are real, and the swap test holds — reverse them and the sentence stops making sense, which is what proves the contrast isn't decorative. Ends on "does." But see the claim check below; this one costs you something.

---

**Recommendation: #1.**

The register table is blunt about taglines — compression usually beats ornament — and #1 is the only treatment that keeps "enterprise architecture" doing its job as the category word, which a platform tagline needs to survive on its own. It has nothing to push against and doesn't need anything; the contrast is with the twenty-two-word sentence it replaces.

Take **#2** instead if the line always appears where the category is already established — a product page, a slide with the logo on it. There the shape earns its keep, and "you" puts a person in the sentence.
