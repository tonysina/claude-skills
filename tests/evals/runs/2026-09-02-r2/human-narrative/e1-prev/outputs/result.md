## The revised case study

Ridgeline Logistics: From Spreadsheet Chaos to Coordinated Scheduling

**The challenge**

Ridgeline Logistics runs 140 delivery vans across the Pacific Northwest. Until 2025, dispatch scheduling lived in a set of spreadsheets maintained by three regional coordinators. Every morning began the same way: coordinators phoned each other to reconcile overlapping routes, and drivers waited in the yard until the calls ended. On a typical day, vans left forty minutes late. Operations director Maria Chen felt the frustration in her chest every time she walked past the idle vans. If you have ever kept a crew waiting while three people reconcile a schedule by phone, you know what those forty minutes cost.

**The solution**

In March 2025, Ridgeline rolled out a shared scheduling platform across all three regions. Coordinators now see one live board. Route conflicts are flagged automatically, and drivers get their assignments on their phones the night before. The rollout took six weeks, including two weeks of parallel running with the old spreadsheets.

**The results**

Within the first quarter, average morning departure delay fell from forty minutes to six. Fuel spend dropped 11 percent because routes stopped overlapping. Coordinator overtime, which had run at roughly twelve hours a week per person, fell to under two.

"Honestly, I didn't expect the drivers to take to it so quickly," Chen said. "By the second week they were asking why we hadn't done this years ago."

**Looking ahead**

Ridgeline plans to extend the platform to its maintenance scheduling in 2026.

## What I flagged instead of executing

**F — name something.** This is the highest value-per-effort change available and I can't make it, because every honest version of it requires a fact you have and I don't. The most obvious one: **the case study never names the platform.** "A shared scheduling platform" is the diffuse-reference move in its purest form — a customer story about an unnamed product. Name it. Second option: name what it replaced, if the spreadsheets ran on something specific. Third: if Ridgeline's rollout was modelled on a named practice, a published operations study, or a specific prior deployment they looked at, one clause naming it moves reference explicitness from none to explicit-named. Whatever you pick has to be checkable — "industry best practice" or "operators report" is vague attribution and makes the piece worse, not more human.

## What I did not take, and why

- **B did not fire.** "Felt the frustration in her chest" is the one embodied beat, and it's the line most people would point at as the AI tell. But it's a third of the emotional beats, there's no olfactory imagery, the yard and the vans aren't mirroring anyone's mood, and interior access is shallow throughout. A gate needs a corroborator and this one has none. I'd leave the line alone; if a later editing pass adds more body-based emotion, the cluster comes back into range.
- **C1 did not fire.** The resolution is external — software went in, the numbers moved. Its two AI-side corroborators (tight causal chain, Chen introduced by job title and description) don't fire a cluster on their own. There's also a truth constraint here: I can't invent an open thread in a case study, and I don't know of one.
