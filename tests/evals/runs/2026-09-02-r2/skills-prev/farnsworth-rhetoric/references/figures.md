# Extended figure catalog

Figures beyond the core set in SKILL.md. Lower frequency in business and thought
leadership writing, but each has a clean trigger. Same rules apply: no trigger, no
figure, and everything here counts against the budget in SKILL.md.

Organized by Farnsworth's families: repetition, structure, address, and voice.

---

## Figures of repetition

### Symploce — anaphora and epistrophe together

**Trigger:** A passage long enough for the ear to tire of one repetition pattern (roughly
four or more parallel clauses).

**Move:** Repeat at the start *and* the end of consecutive clauses, or shift from one to
the other mid-passage. The shift refreshes the ear.

Churchill: "giving all, daring all, enduring all to the utmost, to the end."
Epistrophe (*all, all, all*) shifting into anaphora (*to the..., to the...*).

**Cap:** 1 per piece, long-form only. Counts as one figure. Do not attempt in anything
under 600 words — there isn't enough runway for the shift to register as a shift rather
than as inconsistency.

---

### Anadiplosis — last word becomes the first

**Trigger:** A causal or escalating chain where each step genuinely follows from the last.

**Move:** End a clause on a word, then open the next clause with it.

"Fear leads to anger. Anger leads to hate. Hate leads to suffering."

Before: "Missed deadlines damage trust, and low trust means slower decisions, which
causes more missed deadlines."
After: "Missed deadlines cost trust. Trust is what makes decisions fast. Fast decisions
are what keep deadlines."

**Warning:** This figure asserts causation structurally — the form itself claims that each
step produces the next. If the chain is correlational or merely associative, the figure
lies for you. Run the claim check hard here.

**Cap:** 1 per piece. Three links maximum; four reads as a chant.

---

### Epizeuxis — immediate repetition

**Trigger:** One word is the entire point, and emphasis by any other means would
understate it.

**Move:** Repeat it immediately, with no intervening words.

"Location, location, location."

**Cap:** 1 per piece, and almost never in professional writing. In a memo it reads as
shouting. Reserve for spoken delivery, where the pause between repetitions does the work
that punctuation can't.

## Figures of structure

### Asyndeton — conjunctions removed

**Trigger:** A list where the items are of one kind and the speed matters more than the
relationship between them.

**Move:** Drop the conjunctions. Use commas alone.

Caesar: "I came, I saw, I conquered." (Not "I came, I saw, and I conquered.")

**Effect:** Speed, and an implication that the list could continue. It sounds decisive
because it doesn't pause to organize itself.

**Cap:** Free when used once. It's a punctuation choice more than a figure — count it
against the budget only if the list is the sentence's main event.

---

### Polysyndeton — conjunctions added

**Trigger:** A list where the accumulation is the point — you want each item to land
separately and the total to feel heavy.

**Move:** Put a conjunction before every item.

"And the rain descended, and the floods came, and the winds blew."

Before: "The migration touched billing, provisioning, support tooling, and reporting."
After: "The migration touched billing and provisioning and support tooling and reporting."

**Effect:** Weight and exhaustion. The opposite of asyndeton, from the same list.

**Cap:** 1 per piece. Read it aloud before keeping it — polysyndeton fails badly on the
page when it isn't earned, because a reader who doesn't hear the rhythm just sees bad
grammar.

---

### Praeteritio — mentioning by refusing to mention

**Trigger:** Something the audience already knows, where restating it would insult them
but ignoring it would seem evasive.

**Move:** Name it as the thing you're not going to dwell on.

"I won't rehearse last quarter's numbers. Everyone here has read them."

**Effect:** Raises the item while claiming to set it aside — which is why it can read as
passive-aggressive if the item is a criticism. Use it for shared context, not for
grievances.

**Cap:** 1 per piece.

---

### Litotes — affirmation by understating the negative

**Trigger:** A claim strong enough that stating it plainly would sound like boasting or
alarmism.

**Move:** Negate the opposite. "Not a small problem." "No accident." "Not unwelcome."

Before: "The results were excellent."
After: "The results were not what we'd feared."

**Effect:** Credibility through restraint. Especially useful when the audience is
predisposed to discount enthusiasm.

**Warning:** Litotes and hedging look identical on the page and read very differently in
the ear. "Not insignificant" is litotes; "not necessarily insignificant" is a hedge with
a figure draped over it. If the negation stacks, you're hedging.

**Cap:** 1 per piece.

## Figures of voice

### Strategic passive voice

**Trigger:** The active version separates two words that should be adjacent, or puts the
weak element in the strong final position.

**Move:** Use the passive deliberately, and be able to say why.

Churchill (actual): "Never in the field of human conflict was so much owed by so many to
so few."

Hypothetical active version — **Farnsworth's teaching foil, not a line Churchill wrote:**
"Never have so many owed so much to so few."

The passive puts "so many to so few" adjacent, so the contrast between the two quantities
lands as one motion. The active separates them and the antithesis dissipates.

**Rule:** Never avoid the passive reflexively, and never reach for it reflexively either.
The test is whether you can name what the passive buys you in that specific sentence. If
you can't, use the active.

**Cap:** Free. This is a voice decision, not an ornament.

---

### Direct address

**Trigger:** The reader is being asked to do something, or the piece has drifted into
abstraction and needs a person in it.

**Move:** Address the reader as "you," once, at the moment of the ask.

**Note:** `human-narrative` treats direct address as a structural humanizing signal (human
writers use it at roughly four times the AI rate) and will sometimes recommend adding it.
That recommendation is upstream of this skill — if human-narrative already placed a direct
address, don't add a second one for rhetorical effect.

**Cap:** 1 per piece.

---

## Figures deliberately excluded

Farnsworth catalogs these; they are omitted from this skill on purpose.

| Figure | Why excluded |
|---|---|
| Apostrophe (addressing the absent) | Reads as theatrical in any professional register. |
| Personification | Overlaps with the promotional register humanizer strips. |
| Hyperbole | Almost always fails the claim check in SKILL.md. |
| Aposiopesis (breaking off mid-sentence) | Depends on delivery; unreadable on the page. |
| Prolepsis (anticipating objection) | Covered better by hypophora, which answers rather than pre-empts. |

If a user names one of these directly, apply it — but say which of the above concerns
applies before you do.
