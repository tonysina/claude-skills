Why your card processor's headline rate is not what you pay

The rate a payment processor quotes you is made of three parts, and the processor only sets one of them.

The largest part is the interchange fee, which goes to the bank that issued your customer's card. Visa and Mastercard publish these rates, and they vary by card type: a debit card tap is cheap, a corporate rewards card is not. Your processor cannot discount interchange because it never keeps it.

The second part is the card network's own assessment, a smaller fixed percentage that goes to Visa or Mastercard rather than to any bank.

The third part is the processor's margin. This is the only part under negotiation, and it is usually the smallest of the three.

That is why two merchants on identical contracts can pay different effective rates. A shop whose customers mostly tap debit cards pays close to the headline number. A B2B supplier whose customers pay on corporate credit cards pays substantially more, on the same contract, because their mix sits higher up the interchange table.

A separate question, which comes up whenever this one does: whether you may pass the fee on to the customer as a surcharge. In the UK you may not, for consumer cards, under the Payment Services Regulations 2017. In much of the US you may, subject to card-scheme caps and a handful of state laws that still prohibit it. That rule has nothing to do with how the fee is composed, and merchants routinely conflate the two.

Ask a processor for an effective rate on your own last three months of transactions rather than a headline number. The headline compares the part that varies least.
